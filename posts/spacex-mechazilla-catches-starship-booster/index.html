SpaceX Catches the Starship Booster Mid-Air with Mechazilla

On October 13, 2024, a 71-metre rocket booster returned from space and was caught by mechanical arms on the launch tower — a first in the history of rocketry.

By Editorial Team October 14, 2024  ·  5 min read
#SpaceX#Starship#Rockets#Space#Reusability

At 8:25 a.m. local time on October 13, 2024, SpaceX launched Integrated Flight Test 5 of Starship from the Starbase facility in Boca Chica, Texas. Seven minutes later, the Super Heavy booster — 71 metres tall and one of the largest rockets ever built — returned to the launch site and was caught in mid-air by the mechanical arms of the Mechazilla tower, known formally as the Mechazilla Catch System or "chopstick" arms. No booster of this size has ever been caught before. Every previous large rocket has either landed on legs or been expended.

The catch was the culmination of years of engineering work on what SpaceX calls rapid reusability. The logic is straightforward: landing legs add mass and complexity, require inspection, and take time to service. Catching the booster with the tower arms eliminates all of that. In principle, a caught booster could be inspected, refuelled, and relaunched within hours rather than days. Whether SpaceX achieves that turnaround in practice remains to be seen — but the engineering constraint has been removed.

What IFT-5 Achieved

The Starship upper stage — the vehicle itself, sitting atop the booster — continued its flight to sub-orbital trajectory, crossing the Kármán line and performing a controlled reentry over the Indian Ocean before splashing down as planned. Both stages completed their primary objectives. The flight lasted approximately 65 minutes from launch to Starship splashdown.

The booster catch alone changes the economics of the whole program. You are no longer throwing away a building-sized machine each flight.

The Road to Full Reusability

Each Super Heavy booster is built with 33 Raptor engines and represents hundreds of millions of dollars of hardware. If the booster can be caught, inspected quickly, and reflown — similar to how SpaceX already operates Falcon 9 first stages — the per-launch cost of Starship drops dramatically. SpaceX's stated ambition is point-to-point Earth transport and, eventually, crewed missions to Mars. Neither is viable at expendable rocket prices. Rapid reusability is not an optional feature; it is the business model.

What Comes Next

Integrated Flight Test 6 followed in November 2024, with SpaceX attempting to catch the Starship upper stage as well. The long-term manifest includes Starship missions for NASA's Artemis programme — Starship is the chosen Human Landing System for the Moon — as well as Starlink satellite launches at a fraction of the cost of Falcon 9, and Elon Musk's stated goal of placing humans on Mars within the decade. IFT-5's booster catch was the technical proof point that the architecture is viable at full scale.
